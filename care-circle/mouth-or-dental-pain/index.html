Possible mouth or dental pain

Spot eating mechanics that suggest oral discomfort, protect nutrition, and prepare a safer dental conversation.

Why this matters for Mabel

Mabel is 10 years, a Labrador Retriever, with periodontal disease; the owner reports drops kibble, chews on one side, and takes longer to finish.

Watch how the meal changes

You will capture chewing mechanics rather than only the amount eaten.

The way food is picked up, chewed, dropped, and swallowed can reveal a useful pattern.

  1. Film the first minute of an ordinary meal without opening the mouth by force.
  2. Record side preference, dropped food, pawing, drooling, odor, swallowing, and time to finish.
  3. Measure food and water offered and consumed, plus any weight change.
Which detail best describes mouth function?

Protect food and comfort

You will support intake without giving unsafe medication or masking a worsening problem.

Ask the veterinary team about an appropriate temporary texture and keep a close intake record.

  1. Offer the normal diet in the safest texture your veterinary team recommends.
  2. Keep water easy to reach and record actual intake and vomiting.
  3. Avoid hard chews, mouth handling, human pain medicine, and leftover antibiotics.
What is the safest next step?

Recognize signs that should not wait

You will know when reduced eating has become an urgent pain, airway, or hydration problem.

Facial swelling, uncontrolled bleeding, trauma, inability to swallow, breathing trouble, or complete refusal of food and water needs faster help.

  1. Photograph visible facial swelling without pressing it.
  2. Bring the meal video, intake totals, medicines, and timing of the first change.
  3. Seek urgent care for breathing trouble, inability to swallow, major trauma, severe bleeding, or collapse.
Which sign should not wait?

Prepare an eating and mouth-comfort summary

Connect food texture, chewing behavior and mouth signs to the daily change.

Owners can safely record visible behavior, while a veterinary oral examination determines what is happening.

  1. Record food texture, chewing side, dropped food, meal duration, drooling, pawing and odor.
  2. Photograph only what is visible without forcing the mouth open or touching a painful area.
  3. Share appetite, weight, swelling, bleeding, medicines and the first date the pattern appeared.
